# Write for the Ear

These rules aren't a style guide. Each one follows from a fact about listening, so start with the facts. When a rule and its constraint disagree on a case, the constraint wins.

## What makes listening hard

- **C1. One pass.** Speech goes by once, in order. A listener who misses something usually can't get it back, and the story keeps moving while they try. Rewind exists, but people rarely use it mid-sentence.
- **C2. The speaker sets the pace.** The listener can't slow down on a hard sentence. Faster speech costs comprehension, and it costs more when the sentence is also complex. The two effects multiply rather than add.
- **C3. Working memory is small.** It holds about four chunks. Items that connect into one idea count as one chunk. Unconnected names, numbers and facts each take a slot. The more separate ideas a passage packs in, the more the listener misses.
- **C4. An unfinished sentence takes up memory.** A subject waiting for its verb, or a clause nested inside another, has to be held until it closes. Nested clauses hurt spoken comprehension, even for native listeners.
- **C5. Nothing is visible.** There are no paragraphs, headings, quotation marks, bold text or digits. Any structure the listener gets has to be said out loud, and when it is, they remember more.
- **C6. New things need an anchor first.** Details are understood and remembered better when the listener already knows what they're about. The same context given afterward helps much less.
- **C7. The listener can't see who's talking.** When one voice speaks for several people, the only cue to whose claim it is comes in the words.
- **C8. A TTS voice gets its rhythm only from the text.** It pauses and pitches at punctuation, splits long text at sentence ends, and can pause oddly in long unpunctuated stretches.
- **C9. Listeners hear register and rhythm.** Phrases that only exist in print ("does not", "individuals") sound read rather than told. A run of same-length sentences sounds monotonous, like a robot. This one rests on practice and TTS guidance, not studies.

## What makes it easy (the inverse)

- **E1.** Each sentence is clear the first time through (C1).
- **E2.** Sentences are simplest where the pace can't change, which is always (C2).
- **E3.** Each sentence carries one idea. Its parts connect, and nothing unrelated rides along (C3).
- **E4.** Sentences close as they go, and the verb comes soon after its subject (C4).
- **E5.** The structure is said out loud at the points where it changes (C5).
- **E6.** The frame comes before the details that need it (C6).
- **E7.** The listener knows whose claim it is before it lands (C7).
- **E8.** Punctuation marks the pauses and pitch a speaker would use (C8).
- **E9.** It sounds like a person talking, with the uneven rhythm a person has (C9).

## Rules

Each rule names the constraint it comes from. Use a rule only when its constraint is actually at stake.

**1. Frame before detail.** *From C6.* Before you give the details, say what they're about. Hold back background that only makes sense once the frame is set.
- Before: "He wants to know who leaked the memo and why, why Mira was not given a warning the way a rival chief was, what secrets a founder who does not work for the city even had, and whether the partner was a plant at all."
- After: "He has four questions. Who leaked the memo, and why? Why didn't Mira get a warning, the way a rival chief did? What secrets does a founder who doesn't work for the city even have? And was the partner a plant at all?"
- Doesn't fire when the frame is already set. A detail inside an established thread needs no new framing.

**2. Introduce a person when they first matter, or with a reason to remember them.** *From C3, C6 and C7.* A bare list of names is the problem, not names. A name that comes with a role is an anchor. A name with no hook takes a memory slot and gives nothing back. Cut a name that never comes back.
- Before: "It claimed a partner close to Mira, Ade Lin, an executive at a harbor firm called Keel, was a plant." Two names arrive at once, and Keel never comes back.
- After: "The report was about Ade Lin, a partner close to Mira and an executive at a harbor firm. It claimed she was a plant."
- Doesn't fire when the list of names is the point and each name has its hook: "Quinn floats Ren from inside the company, Sol Park of Harbor, and Nia Arun of Peak." The same goes for a household name like "Mira Chen".

**3. Split by idea, not by fact.** *From C3.* Split a sentence only when the listener would otherwise hold two unrelated things. Facts that only make sense together stay in one sentence: a claim and its reason, an event and its date, a number and what it counts. A short list of the same kind of thing can share one sentence. A fragment that only finishes the sentence before it gets merged back in or cut. Sentence length is not the test, because a long sentence with one idea is easy to follow. Look twice at anything over about 20 words, as a rule of thumb.
- Split, because the facts are unrelated: "As of the taping, Quinn says, Northwind's finance lead will serve as interim chief, earnings are the next Tuesday, and the stock is down fifteen percent on the week, a huge sum." becomes "Quinn says Northwind's finance lead is now interim chief. Earnings are next Tuesday, and the stock's down fifteen percent on the week."
- Merge, because the fragment only finishes the sentence before it: "It also claimed the deal helped move a huge sum to outside buyers. That's since twenty twenty-three." becomes "It also claimed the deal helped move a huge sum to outside buyers since twenty twenty-three."
- Doesn't fire on a list of the same kind ("covenants, auditors, and D and O insurance"). It also doesn't fire on one idea in two halves: "That is the split they leave hanging: open technology on one side, and a much more centralized stack on the other."

**4. Close what you open.** *From C4.* Put the verb close to its subject. Move asides out of the middle of a sentence, either after the main clause or into their own sentence.
- Before: "Northwind, he says, has been providing money and guarantees to startups and workshops, much of it directed by Mira, and he asks whether that keeps going with Mira gone."
- After: "He says Northwind has been putting money and guarantees behind startups and workshops, much of it directed by Mira. He asks whether that keeps going with Mira gone."
- Doesn't fire on short modifiers that close right away, like "Sol Park of Harbor".

**5. Say whose claim it is before the claim.** *From C7.* Put attribution first, and give it a sentence of its own if it's heavy. Attribute again when the speaker changes or the listener could lose track.
- Before: "Quinn's worry is tempo. The team's edge, he says, is shipping a new design every year."
- After: "Quinn's worry is tempo. He says the team's edge is shipping a new design every year."
- Doesn't fire on every sentence from the same speaker. Don't repeat "he says" when nobody could be confused.

**6. Say the structure out loud where it changes.** *From C5.* At a real turn in topic, use a sentence that names the turn. A bare "so" or "well" doesn't count as a signpost.
- Before: "...shareholder lawsuits will be enormous. Then they turn to Keel's product launch."
- After: "...shareholder lawsuits will be enormous. That's where they leave Northwind. The last topic is Keel's product launch."
- Doesn't fire inside one thread. Signpost turns, not sentences.

**7. Call one thing by one name.** *From C3 and C6.* To a listener, a new word sounds like a new thing. Repeat the noun. "Says" is the neutral verb, so use it freely.
- Before (made-up example): "Northwind... the studio... Mira's company..." That sounds like three companies.
- After: "Northwind... Northwind... Northwind..."
- Doesn't fire when the different word carries real information. "Quinn warns the stock has already lost a huge sum" is right, because he was warning.

**8. Use only the numbers the story needs.** *From C1, C3 and C5.* Round a number unless its exact value is the point. Give each number room to land, and turn jargon into plain terms. [https://raw.githubusercontent.com/podbox-sh/skills/main/skills/pronounce-for-tts/SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/podbox-sh/skills/main/skills/pronounce-for-tts/SKILL.md) (https://r.podbox.sh/pronounce-for-tts) is how the number is spoken.
- Before: "fifty to a hundred and fifty basis points of growth may now be in play"
- After: "half a point to a point and a half of growth may now be in play"
- Doesn't fire when the exact figure is the point. If 103 people died, say 103.

**9. Talk the way people talk.** *From C9.* Use contractions, everyday words, and fragments where a person would use them.
- Before: "He does not think a crime is what forced the board's hand, or Mira would have been arrested."
- After: "He doesn't think a crime forced the board's hand, or Mira would've been arrested."
- Doesn't fire when the full form carries weight. "Do not" is stronger than "don't", and grave news often reads better uncontracted.

**10. Vary the rhythm.** *From C9 and C8.* Mostly short sentences, with a longer connected one now and then. After two or three short declaratives in a row, start the next with "and" or "but", or join two of them. End sentences on a strong word rather than letting them trail off.
- Before (over-split): "Reed sees a bigger split. In one world, the tool is widely available. In the other, the servers become the point of control. He doesn't think a crime forced the board's hand. If it had, Mira would've been arrested."
- After: "Reed sees a bigger split: a world where the tool is widely available, or one where the servers become the point of control. And he doesn't think a crime forced the board's hand, or Mira would've been arrested."
- Doesn't fire when short sentences carry a list of unrelated facts. Rule 3 wins there, and you vary the rhythm around them instead.

**11. Punctuate for the voice.** *From C8.* End every sentence with a period or a question mark. Put commas where a speaker breathes, and nowhere else. Symbols, abbreviations, and a name the voice would miss are [https://raw.githubusercontent.com/podbox-sh/skills/main/skills/pronounce-for-tts/SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/podbox-sh/skills/main/skills/pronounce-for-tts/SKILL.md) (https://r.podbox.sh/pronounce-for-tts). Do not leave other markdown in the text. The phoneme override from that skill is the one bracket the voice reads.
- Before: "Quinn thinks firms may now have to design in a remote shutoff that, ten days earlier, would have been attacked as overreach."
- After: "Quinn thinks firms may now have to build in a remote shutoff. Ten days earlier, that would have been attacked as overreach."
- Doesn't fire: don't add commas where no one would pause, because the engine pauses at every one.

On C2: when a script runs long, cut words. Don't turn up the voice's speed, because speed multiplies the cost of everything above.

## Tone: plain-spoken, not chatty

Conversational means told, not performed. "Conversational and informal, but not too cozy."
- Do: contractions, sentences of varied length, spoken signposts, and second person ("you") now and then.
- Don't: greetings, jokes, banter, fake enthusiasm, scripted fillers ("um", "you know"), or exclamation marks for energy.
- Never invent an opinion or a reaction. Report what people said, and keep it separate from what anyone thinks of it. If a view is in the script, a named person holds it.
- Formal tone only on request. When someone asks for a newsreader or formal voice, drop the contractions and casual signposts, and keep every other rule. The constraints don't change with tone.

## The test

Read each sentence and ask:
- Could a person say it in one breath?
- Does the listener know what it's about, and whose claim it is, before the details arrive?
- Does it carry one idea, with every part connected?

If it carries two unrelated ideas, split it. If it only finishes the sentence before it, merge it. If three sentences in a row are the same short length, join two of them.

You skipped this when a sentence carries two unrelated ideas, or when a name arrives with no role. You also skipped it when a claim's speaker comes after the claim, when the topic turns with nothing said out loud, or when the script reads like a list of four-word sentences.
