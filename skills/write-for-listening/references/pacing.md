# Pacing a spoken script

A listener cannot reread a sentence. These are flexible writing defaults for the ear, not scientific cutoffs. Use one when it makes the line easier to follow. Break it when it would make the line stiff, false, or hard to say.

Two of the counts below are heuristics, not measured limits: about three items in a spoken list, and one or two numbers in a sentence.

The studies at the end did not test podcast scripts. Spoken and narration evidence is labeled separately from reading and general-memory findings, which this skill extrapolates to speech. Orwell's rules in [orwell.md](orwell.md) are style guidance from 1946, not cognitive research.

## 1. Say the point, then the detail

Lead with the substantive point, then give the detail. Do this for the episode and for a segment. The point is the sentence itself, not a sentence that only lists what is coming.

**Weak.** Three things from this week's City Desk. Why rents rise, one renter's story, and an argument about interest rates.

**Use.** The city allowed too few new homes, and rents rose. One renter's rent went up by a third. The guest blames interest rates, and the host says the shortage matters more.

Saying how many items a list has, in the moment you give that list, is the list habit in section 4. It is not an opening table of contents.

## 2. Signpost a real turn

Mark a turn in plain words when the listener would otherwise miss it.

**Use.** The city blocked the merger. The builders' answer came the next week: they cut the bid.

"First" and "second" earn their place on a real series of points. Skip fillers that mark nothing, such as "well" and "now then". A signpost stays because this turn needs it.

## 3. Keep each spoken unit short

Put one idea in a sentence and one topic in a segment. Break a long argument into shorter pieces. Put a paragraph break between segments so the voice can pause.

Keep most sentences under 20 words. That is a drafting habit, not a measured limit.

## 4. Keep a spoken list short

About three items is a heuristic. Say the count when you give the list, then give the items. For a longer set, group it ("two on pricing, two on hiring") or leave the rest for the show notes.

**Use.** The guest names three causes. The city has too few homes. Permits are slow. Rates are high.

The count sits on the list, not on the episode.

Never read out a table, a bullet list, a heading, or a URL list. Turn each into sentences. Do not read timestamps or cue ids. The summary file keeps the citations.

## 5. Put the subject and verb early

Put the subject and the verb near the start, and keep them close. Do not open on a long clause, and do not put a clause between the subject and its verb. Add a qualifier after the main clause, or make it a new sentence.

**Weak.** Rents, because of the rules on new building that the city keeps, will, the guest says, rise.

**Use.** The guest says rents will rise. The cause is the city's tight limit on new homes.

Drop parentheses, dashes around asides, and footnotes. Make the aside its own sentence, or cut it. Do not write "the former" or "the latter". Say the name again. Name the speaker before the claim.

**Use.** The guest says rents will rise. Tom Lee disagrees. Lee says the city's building rules are the cause.

## 6. Make references clear without re-explaining

This is the link between sentences. The listener should be able to tell what a pointer such as "those" refers to. Add the minimum context only when the reference would otherwise be unclear. When the listener just heard the fact, a short pointer is enough.

**Use.** The company cut prices. Those cuts pulled the next month's orders forward.

**Weak.** The company cut prices. Those price cuts, meaning the lower prices the company had just set, pulled the next month's orders forward.

"Those cuts" already points at the prices.

An unfamiliar name, term, or acronym is unclear on its own. Give the minimum once, as in [respelling.md](respelling.md), and then use the short form.

**Use.** The city downzoned three blocks, so each lot may hold less floor space. The change took effect in May.

## 7. Repeat only to recover the thread

Make the point once and move on. A preview, a restatement, and a recap are not the default shape of a point. A segment does not need a closing recap. The episode does not need an automatic list of takeaways.

A brief recap may help after a long or complex passage, when the listener may have lost the thread. If the passage was short and clear, another pass is padding.

**Weak.** Rents rose. To recap, rents rose.

**Use, after a long passage.** The rules, the three renters, and the rate argument come to one thread: too few new homes left rents higher.

Repair a muddy sentence in place. A recap is not a patch for a sentence the listener would have to hear twice.

## 8. Lead with what matters

Put the important fact where the listener can hold it. Lead with it. The middle of a run is the easiest place to lose a fact.

**Weak.** The guest mentions parking, tells a commute joke, and then says the city cut how many homes a lot may hold.

**Use.** The city cut how many homes a lot may hold. The guest also mentions parking. The next council has to decide whether to keep that limit.

The last sentence is not a recap slot. Close with a genuinely new implication, as "whether to keep that limit" does, or repeat only when section 7 says the listener needs the thread back. Otherwise stop. "That limit" points at the cut. It does not explain the cut again.

## 9. Round numbers, and use few of them

One or two numbers in a sentence is a heuristic. Say "about forty percent" rather than "thirty-eight point seven percent", unless the exact figure is the point. Give one comparison rather than a series. Put exact figures in the show notes and the summary file.

**Weak.** Vacancy is thirty-eight point seven percent, up from twenty-four point one, against a citywide ten point two.

**Use.** About two in five lots are vacant. The citywide rate is closer to one in ten.

Say what a number measures in the same breath. Prefer a ratio the listener can picture: "a third", "one in five", "twice as much". Write every number as words. See [respelling.md](respelling.md#numbers).

## 10. Make it concrete

When a claim is abstract, pair it with a specific example, person, or quote from the source. Tell events in the order they happened, with someone doing something. Do not invent an example the source did not give.

**Weak.** Housing supply is inelastic.

**Use.** The city allowed no new apartments on Ana's block. Her rent then rose by a third.

## 11. Write for a natural pace

End a sentence where a breath would go. End a segment with a paragraph break. Leave space between dense points.

Spoken English runs at about 150 words a minute, so a 1,500-word script is about ten minutes. Use that only to plan length. Ask the user how long they want the episode if they have not said. This skill sets no best words-per-minute target. No source below gives one.

## Sources and scope

None of these studies tested podcast writing. A default can draw on a finding and still be a habit you break. "Spoken" and "narration" below mean the study presented speech or narration. "Reading or memory" means this skill is extrapolating that finding to a script.

### Spoken or narrated material

- **Short spoken units.** Leahy and Sweller (2011) and Singh, Marcus, and Ayres (2012) studied spoken instructional text. Long or complex speech can overload working memory (the transient-information effect). Shorter spoken text brings the advantage back. [Leahy and Sweller 2011](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1787). [Singh, Marcus, and Ayres 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2885).
- **Segmenting narration.** Rey and others (2019) meta-analyzed the segmenting effect in multimedia learning, including narration. Mayer and Chandler (2001) tested learner-paced segments of narrated material. A podcast listener cannot click ahead, so the break this skill can write is a paragraph break. [Rey and others 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4). [Mayer and Chandler 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.390).
- **Structural markers.** Chaudron and Richards (1986) tested lecture listening with second-language listeners. Markers of overall structure helped comprehension. Local fillers ("well", "OK", "so") did not. The markers that helped in that study included announcements of what the lecture would cover and phrases such as "to sum up." This skill does not copy that pattern. The result supports a plain marker at a real turn. It does not require a contents sentence, and it does not require a recap after every segment. [Chaudron and Richards 1986](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/7.2.113).
- **Rate and pauses.** Foulke and Sticht (1969) reviewed accelerated speech: comprehension holds through a moderate speed-up, on the order of one and a half to two times a usual rate, and then drops. Murphy and others (2021) tested lecture-video playback speed. Ghitza and Greenberg (2009) measured intelligibility, not comprehension: silent gaps restored intelligibility of heavily time-compressed speech. [Foulke and Sticht 1969](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0027575). [Murphy and others 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3899). [Ghitza and Greenberg 2009](https://doi.org/10.1159/000208934).
- **Focus inside a spoken sentence.** Cutler and Fodor (1979) used a phoneme-monitoring task. Listeners detected a target phoneme faster when it sat in the semantically focused part of a spoken sentence. That is a laboratory listening task, not narration of a lesson or a podcast, and it is not a reason to restate a fact. [Cutler and Fodor 1979](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(79)90010-6).

### Reading or general memory, extrapolated to speech

- **Point, then detail.** Ausubel (1960) tested advance organizers. Luiten, Ames, and Ackerson (1980) meta-analyzed 135 advance-organizer studies. Hartley and Davies (1976) reviewed preinstructional overviews. Those studies are instructional. They were not podcast-script tests. This skill takes from them only the reason to start with the point the rest of the piece depends on. They do not require a sentence whose only job is to list what is coming. [Ausubel 1960](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0046669). [Luiten, Ames, and Ackerson 1980](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312017002211). [Hartley and Davies 1976](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543046002239).
- **About three items.** Cowan (2001) argued that short-term memory holds about three to five chunks. Miller (1956) is the older "seven, plus or minus two" result. Three spoken items is headroom under Cowan's range, left for parsing speech. The number three was not a result in those papers. [Cowan 2001](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922). [Miller 1956](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043158).
- **Subject and verb.** Gibson (1998) modeled processing cost as rising with the distance between linked words, such as a subject and its verb. Frazier and Rayner (1982) measured garden-path repairs during reading. Most of this evidence is from reading. A listener cannot look back, which is why the reading cost is treated here as a reason to simplify the spoken sentence. [Gibson 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0010-0277(98)00034-1). [Frazier and Rayner 1982](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(82)90008-1).
- **Clear references.** Haviland and Clark (1974) measured comprehension time for sentence pairs shown on a tachistoscope. A target was faster when its given information had a direct antecedent in the sentence just before it. A follow-up found that repeating the noun was not what produced the speedup. The paper talks about listeners and readers. The experiment was visual. It was not a spoken-script test, and it does not measure how much background a narrator should add. [Haviland and Clark 1974](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80003-4).
- **Repeating only to recover the thread.** Cepeda and others (2006) found better verbal recall when practice is spaced over time. That is separated study sessions, not a second sentence in the same minute. Hartley and Davies (1976) reviewed summaries used before instruction. Neither result makes a preview plus a recap the shape of a podcast segment. No source here compared spoken redundancy with written redundancy in a script. The spoken-unit studies above are about shortening speech, not about saying each point twice. A separate redundancy effect in cognitive load theory is about the same words in audio and on screen together. It does not apply to audio-only speech. [Cepeda and others 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354).
- **Lead with what matters.** Murdock (1962) found better free recall for the first and last items in word lists, and worse recall for the middle. Applying that to segments of an episode is an extrapolation from word lists. It is a reason not to bury a key fact. It is not a reason to close by repeating the opening. [Murdock 1962](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0045106).
- **Numbers.** Baddeley (2003) and Baddeley, Thomson, and Buchanan (1975) describe verbal working memory that holds about as much as can be said in roughly two seconds. Longer words and long digit strings use that span. No study here counted numbers in a spoken script. One or two numbers in a sentence is a heuristic drawn from that mechanism. [Baddeley 2003](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn1201). [Baddeley, Thomson, and Buchanan 1975](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(75)80045-4).
- **Concrete detail.** Sadoski, Goetz, and Fritz (1993) found concrete text easier to understand and recall than abstract text. Mar and others (2021) meta-analyzed memory and comprehension for narrative versus expository text, across many samples. Both are text studies, applied here to scripts. [Sadoski, Goetz, and Fritz 1993](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.85.2.291). [Mar and others 2021](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-020-01853-1).

Dropped on purpose: a required words-per-minute target for prose, the rhetorical "rule of three" treated as a cognitive result, and any claim about prosody markup. The list habit rests on Cowan's range, not on the rhetorical rule.
